Before You Hire a Designer: 10 Website Changes You Can Safely Make Yourself

This is not an argument against designers. It is an argument against paying someone - and waiting a week - to change 16 pixels to 20. There is a line, and it is worth knowing exactly where it is.

First, what a professional is actually for

Pretending that everything on a website is DIY is how sites end up fragile and inaccessible. These remain professional work, and hiring for them is money well spent:

  • Brand systems - a coherent identity, not one nice page
  • Information architecture - what pages exist and how people move between them
  • Responsive strategy - deciding what happens at each breakpoint, not patching afterwards
  • Accessibility and contrast - standards, keyboard behaviour, screen readers
  • Conversion work - testing, measurement, and knowing why a page underperforms
  • Complex builds - custom layouts, integrations, performance budgets

None of that is what most people are waiting for. Most people are waiting for a colour, a spacing value, or an image crop - and those are the ten items below.

Reduce dependency, not eliminate expertise. The goal is to stop queueing for small cosmetic changes, not to pretend the hard parts do not exist.

The ten changes

Each one is visual, reversible and verifiable by looking - which is the test for whether you should be doing it yourself.

1. Change a colour

A button, a link, a heading, a section background. The value is one line, the result is immediate, and if you do not like it you change it back. The only caution is contrast: light grey on white looks elegant and fails readability.

2. Adjust corner radius

Cards, buttons and images. Four values cover the whole range: 0-4px crisp, 6-12px soft, 16-24px friendly, 999px pill. Consistency between them matters more than the number - see 5 Small CSS Changes.

3. Fix cramped or uneven spacing

Inside a card: padding. Between cards: gap or margin. Which is which is the whole of CSS Padding vs. Margin vs. Gap, and it is a five-minute read that saves an invoice.

4. Set content width

Long lines of text are the most common reason a site looks unfinished. One max-width rule on paragraphs fixes it, and it is the highest ratio of visual improvement to effort on this list.

5. Adjust heading sizes and line-height

Heading roughly 1.4 to 1.8 times body size, body line-height around 1.6, headings tighter. These two numbers decide whether a page reads as a document or a poster.

6. Tame or add a shadow

Most default shadows are too heavy. Low opacity, small offset, blur proportional to the element - and on dark backgrounds use a 1px border instead, because dark-on-dark shadows only muddy edges.

7. Crop and resize images

Inconsistent ratios are more damaging than any colour choice. Pick one ratio per context, crop to it, export at the size it will be displayed. See Image Size vs. Aspect Ratio and Resizing Product Photos.

8. Add breathing room to a section

Sections that run into each other look accidental. A consistent vertical padding - one number used everywhere - is most of what separates a considered page from a thrown-together one.

9. Restyle one button

Radius, padding, colour, maybe a shadow. Four values on one selector. This is the single most frequently requested small change on small sites, and it is genuinely a two-minute job once you can see it.

10. Clean up exported or messy markup

If you pasted HTML from a builder, an old site or an assistant, it is probably unreadable and carrying junk. Making it readable without changing the structure is a mechanical job - see How to Clean Up Exported HTML and the HTML Cleaner.

The test for whether you should do it yourself

Ask three questions. If all three are yes, do it yourself:

  1. Can I see whether it worked? If the result is visible immediately, you can judge it. If it only shows up under conditions you cannot create - a screen reader, a slow connection, a specific device - get help.
  2. Can I undo it? One rule on one selector is reversible. A structural change touching several pages is not, unless you have a backup of what was there before.
  3. Is it a value or a structure? Values are taste; structures are engineering. Changing 16px to 20px is a value. Making a two-column layout become three columns at a breakpoint is a structure.

When you do make the change yourself, do it in the order set out in A Safer Workflow for Editing Squarespace CSS: preview, adjust, copy, test. The reason those four steps exist is that they make item two above true.

What doing it yourself actually saves

Not the fee, mostly. The fee for one small change is usually modest. What costs real money is the latency: a week of a page looking wrong, a batch of listing photos waiting on a crop, a seasonal banner that goes live after the season. Ten small changes a year, each waiting a week, is a quarter of the year with a site that is slightly behind.

There is a second, quieter benefit. When you make the change yourself, you know what it was. Six months later, when something looks off, you are not reading someone else's CSS trying to work out which of the twelve rules is responsible.

Where the line moves

Three situations where a change that looks small is not, and DIY stops being sensible:

  • It affects several page types. A rule that reaches blog posts, product pages and the checkout is a scope decision, and getting it wrong is expensive. See Why Copy-Pasting CSS Sometimes Breaks Your Site.
  • It has to work on every screen. Anything involving breakpoints, reflow or mobile navigation is a responsive strategy question.
  • You have already tried twice. Two failed attempts means the problem is not the value - it is the structure, the selector or the theme. That is the moment to stop and ask.

If you do need someone, brief them properly

Half the cost of hiring is usually the brief. Four things make the difference between one round and three:

  • Bring the symptom, not the prescription. "The pricing section feels cramped on mobile" is useful. "Add 24px padding to .summary-item" is you doing their job badly - and if the real cause is a breakpoint, the instruction was wrong anyway.
  • Say what you already tried and what happened. It saves them repeating your attempts, and it often contains the diagnosis.
  • Name the constraint. Timeline, budget, "must not touch the checkout" - the boundaries they cannot see are the ones that get missed.
  • Ask for the explanation, not just the fix. A one-line note on what was wrong means next time you may not need to ask at all. That is the whole point of doing the small ones yourself.

And when the work is done, ask for the CSS to be commented. Uncommented custom CSS is how a site becomes something nobody will touch - including you.

Real scenarios

The studio that stopped queueing. They were waiting on a designer for button radius, section spacing and image crops - three changes a month, each billed and each taking a week. After learning to make those themselves, they still hire the same designer, now for a seasonal layout and a proper accessibility pass. More useful work, less waiting.

The seller who fixed her own grid. Her product grid had uneven gaps because each card carried its own margin. Replacing them with a single gap on the grid took one change and made every row consistent.

The consultant who knew when to stop. He fixed his heading sizes himself, then tried to make his two-column section reflow on mobile and broke it twice. He handed that one over - correctly, because it was a structure question, not a value question.

Questions people ask

Will I break my site?

A one-property change on a narrow selector is very hard to break permanently. The risk grows with breadth: rules that reach many pages, and rules using !important. Keep changes narrow and commented, and keep a copy of what you replaced.

Do I need to learn CSS for this?

No. You need to be able to see the change and undo it. That is the argument behind You Don't Need to Learn CSS to Customize Squarespace: the skill is judgement, and judgement needs a preview, not a syntax course.

Is this taking work away from designers?

It moves it. The work that disappears is the work designers least want - the 16px to 20px ticket. What remains is brand, structure, accessibility and conversion, which is where the value is and where hiring clearly pays.

What if I make it worse?

Remove what you added rather than adding a rule to fight it. Counter-rules are how simple CSS becomes unmaintainable CSS. If you kept a note of the original value, undo is deletion, not archaeology.

The short version

Ten changes - colours, radius, spacing, content width, type scale, shadows, image crops, section padding, button styling, markup cleanup - are visual, reversible and yours to make. Structures, breakpoints, accessibility and brand systems are not.
